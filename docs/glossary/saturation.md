# Saturation (qualitative research)

In qualitative research, saturation is the point at which new interviews repeat themes you already have, rather than adding new ones. It is a judgment about whether more conversations are still changing what you understand, not a fixed interview count for every study. How soon it arrives depends on how focused the question is and how similar the people are.

Updated November 27, 2026.

## How it's used

Researchers use saturation as a stop-and-check, not as a trophy. After each interview, or after a small batch, you compare new stories with the themes you already have. If new interviews keep adding cases of the same theme, and no new theme is appearing that would change your decision, you may be close to saturation for that question and that group of people.

A narrow question with similar people often reaches repetition sooner. A broad question, or a mix of roles and contexts, often takes longer. If you add a new segment, you should expect new themes again.

Saturation is about the quality of understanding for a defined question. It is not a reason to skip [churn](churn-interview.html), [win-loss](win-loss-interview.html), or [discovery](discovery-interview.html) interviews you still need from a different group.

## Example

You are interviewing recently cancelled customers about the trigger behind the cancel. After a run of interviews, every new conversation is another case of the same few triggers: a missing workflow, a committee objection, a switch to a workaround. No new kind of trigger is appearing, and the quotes are starting to sound familiar. That is a sign you may have enough on this question, for this group.

If you then interview a different plan, country, or role, treat that as a new slice. Familiar themes in the first group do not automatically cover the second.

There is no interview number that guarantees saturation for every study, so this page does not give one.

## Common mistakes

- Picking a number in advance and treating it as proof that you are done, whether or not new themes are still appearing.
- Stopping because you are tired, or because the first few people were encouraging.
- Claiming saturation after talking only to one kind of customer, then generalizing to everyone.
- Confusing volume with saturation. A large set of interviews can still miss a segment, and a focused set can repeat early.
