# Win-loss interview

> **TL;DR.** A win-loss interview is a conversation with a buyer from a deal you recently won or lost. The goal is to learn what mattered in the decision, who else was involved, and what almost changed the outcome. It is based on a real closed deal, not a hypothetical purchase or a general review of your category. You invite people from deals already in your CRM or inbox, cover both wins and losses when you can, and leave the conversation as research, not a late attempt to reopen the deal.

## For a small B2B team

A small B2B team already has the raw material for win-loss interviews: closed deals, and the people who evaluated them. What it usually lacks is time, a consistent [interview guide](interview-guide.html), and a way to hear losses with as much care as wins. You do not need a research agency to start. You do need a habit that survives a busy week: reach out soon after the deal closes, invite buyers you already know, ask the same core questions, and write down what the answers change.

Sales-led follow-up calls are the default at this size, and they can still be useful when a teammate who did not own the deal asks the questions. They are a weak default when the salesperson on the deal is the one asking, because buyers often soften criticism in that setting. An async survey after close can tell you how many people named price or a missing feature. It rarely tells you how the committee actually decided, or what almost went the other way. An AI-moderated voice interview, including [Frank AI Researcher](https://www.hifrank.ai/?utm_source=frank-mcp-docs&utm_medium=glossary&utm_campaign=win-loss-interview), is one way to keep a consistent guide and let buyers talk when they have time, without booking a sales follow-up. A third-party interviewer is still the better fit when the deal is sensitive or the story is political.

The rest of this page is a playbook you can run with the deals you already closed, plus a comparison of those approaches and a short FAQ.

## Playbook

### 1. When to reach out

Reach out while the evaluation is still easy to recall, soon after the deal is won or lost. Waiting months makes the tradeoffs harder to reconstruct, and the people who sat on the committee may have changed roles. A small team does not have to wait for a quarterly batch. A short message after each close, or a weekly pass over deals that closed in the last few days, is enough to keep the queue fresh.

Say clearly that the deal is closed and that you want to learn how the decision was made. If the buyer thinks this is a late sales call, they will either decline or give you a polite summary. Wins and losses both belong in the same window. A win that felt easy can still hide a close alternative, a skeptical economic buyer, or a painful implementation surprise. A loss that felt like "price" can turn out to be a champion who could not get time with finance, or a product that did not cover a workflow the incumbent already handled.

There are times to wait a few days rather than write the same afternoon: a messy legal close, a buyer who is clearly exhausted, or a relationship you still need to handle with care. There are also times to skip a live follow-up and send a link instead, so the buyer can talk without finding a slot on a shared calendar. In every case, the clock that matters is the buyer's memory of the evaluation, not your reporting calendar.

### 2. Who to invite

Invite buyers from deals you already won or lost, taken from your own CRM or email list. [Frank AI Researcher](https://www.hifrank.ai/?utm_source=frank-mcp-docs&utm_medium=glossary&utm_campaign=win-loss-interview) does not recruit participants, and a small team should not wait on a panel. Cover both wins and losses when you can. Interviewing only wins, or only the champion who already likes you, leaves out the people who compared you, blocked the deal, or chose something else.

When a buying committee was involved, try to hear more than one seat: the champion who ran the evaluation, the economic buyer who approved spend, and, when they are a different person, someone who would have used the product. Those people often cared about different things. One of them can tell you a clean product story. Another can tell you why legal stalled, or why the incumbent was less risky.

You will not get every seat on every deal, and you do not need a fixed interview count to start. Take the recent closed deals you can already reach, keep the guide consistent, and look at whether new conversations are still changing the picture. If the same three reasons keep showing up in losses, and wins keep confirming the same "almost lost because" themes, you have something you can take to the team. If every interview is a new story, tighten the segment or the question rather than collecting a larger pile of one-off quotes.

Skip people who were not part of the evaluation, and be careful with a contact who only appeared in the last week of a long deal. They can still be worth inviting, but ask who else was involved, rather than treating their account as complete.

### 3. What to ask

Ask about a real evaluation, not a general review of your category. A consistent [interview guide](interview-guide.html) makes comparisons across deals more reliable, as long as you leave room to follow a story. Keep the questions open and non-leading. "Did you choose us because of X?" is a [leading question](leading-question.html). So is "Was price the issue?" The buyer can say yes to both and still leave out the moment the decision actually became clear.

A short guide for a small B2B team can stay close to this pattern:

1. Walk me through how your team evaluated tools like this. What started the search?
2. Who else was involved in the decision, and what did each person care about?
3. What options did you compare, including staying with what you already had?
4. What almost blocked the purchase, or almost made you choose something else?
5. After the decision, what took longer than you expected?

Useful follow-ups stay with the event: "What happened next?", "How did you decide?", "What did that person need to see?", and "If you had to explain the choice to a colleague who missed the meetings, what would you say?" Ask about the incumbent or "do nothing" path in the same tone as you ask about named alternatives. Status quo is often the real comparison, even when a competitor was on the shortlist.

Leave out anything that turns the conversation into a pitch: a demo offer in the middle of a loss interview, a request for a referral before you have heard the story, or a prompt that asks the buyer to rate your features one by one. You can also start from the [customer interview guide generator](../tools/customer-interview-guide-generator/).

### 4. What to do with the answers

The value is in the comparison, not in any single colorful quote. Read each interview against the others, and keep wins and losses in the same set. A simple grid is enough for a small team: what triggered the search, who was on the committee and what they cared about, which options were compared, what almost changed the outcome, and what was harder than expected after the decision.

Look for patterns that would change a decision you can actually make. Product can use "what almost blocked the purchase" and "what took longer after the decision." Sales can use who else had to say yes, and where the evaluation stalled. Marketing can use the words buyers used to describe the problem and the alternatives, which is one input to a [voice of customer](voice-of-customer.html) practice. A one-page readout works better than a long deck: what you heard, one exact quote for each theme, what you will do, and what you will not do on the basis of a single interview.

Check the quotes in the transcripts before they travel. A summary can flatten a political story into "price," or turn a delayed implementation into a product complaint. When a theme shows up in losses and also as a close call in wins, treat that as stronger evidence than a theme that appears once. When the stories keep diverging, say so, rather than forcing a single narrative.

Do not use the interview itself to reopen the deal. If a buyer from a loss wants to restart a conversation, hand that to sales after you have finished learning. The habit that makes win-loss useful at a small company is a short, regular pass from closed deal to invite to readout, not a one-time project.

## Approaches compared

A small B2B team can collect post-decision evidence in several ways. They are not interchangeable. A live call is still the right tool when you need to follow a messy story in real time. An async survey is a fit when you need counts. An AI-moderated voice interview is a fit when you want comparable stories and cannot book every call. A third-party interviewer is a fit when candor or facilitation matters more than speed. Recorded sales calls and CRM notes are useful context, but they capture the sale, not the post-decision interview.

## Common mistakes

- Interviewing only wins, or only the champion who already likes you. Losses and other stakeholders often hold the missing reasons.
- Letting the conversation become a late sales pitch. The interview is for learning, not for reopening the deal in the moment.
- Asking Did you choose us because of X? That is a [leading question](leading-question.html).
- Waiting so long that the people who evaluated the deal have left, or can no longer recall the tradeoffs.
- Treating a CRM reason code, or a single survey checkbox, as the story. Those labels are a starting point for an interview, not the finding.
- Publishing a theme without opening the transcript. A summary can turn a committee delay into "they did not like the product."
- Stopping after two encouraging win interviews and calling that a program. Look at losses, and at whether new conversations are still changing what you think.

## FAQ

### What is the best tool for win/loss interviews for a small B2B team?

There is no single best tool for every team. A small B2B team that already has closed-deal contacts often needs a consistent way to interview those buyers without booking every call. Frank AI Researcher is a voice-first product that runs AI-moderated voice interviews with buyers you invite. It does not recruit participants, and it does not replace a human interviewer when the conversation is relationship-heavy.

### Should the salesperson who closed the deal run the win-loss interview?

Sometimes, but candor can suffer. Buyers often soften criticism when they are talking to the person who sold them. If the sales team runs the interview, it helps if someone who did not own the deal asks the questions, and if the guide stays non-leading. An AI-moderated voice interview can also reduce that pressure, because the interviewer is not the salesperson.

### When should a small team use a third-party interviewer instead?

Use a third-party interviewer when the deal is sensitive, when you need a skilled human to follow a messy political story, or when the buyer is more likely to talk to a named interviewer than to a link. That option usually costs more and takes longer to schedule.

### Can a small B2B team run win-loss interviews without a research agency?

Yes. If you already have closed-deal contacts, you can invite those buyers, use a consistent non-leading guide, and compare themes across wins and losses. A research agency or a third-party interviewer is still useful when the deal is sensitive or you need a skilled human to follow a messy political story. You do not need an agency to start.

### Does Frank recruit buyers for win-loss interviews?

No. Frank AI Researcher does not recruit participants. You invite buyers from deals you already won or lost, for example from your CRM or email list.

### How soon after a deal should I run a win-loss interview?

Run it while the evaluation is still easy to recall, soon after the deal is won or lost. Waiting months makes it harder for people to remember the tradeoffs, and the people who evaluated the deal may have moved on.
