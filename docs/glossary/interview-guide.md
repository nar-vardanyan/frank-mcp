# Interview guide

> **TL;DR.** An interview guide is a planned list of topics or questions for a research interview, usually in a consistent order, with room to follow up. It keeps interviews comparable across people without turning the conversation into a rigid script. A good guide is built around a decision you need to make, not every topic you are curious about. A SaaS founder can write a short one in a single sitting and reuse the same core for churn, win-loss, or discovery interviews with people they can already reach.

Narek Vardanyan, CEO of Frank AI Researcher. Updated November 27, 2026.

## For a SaaS founder

If you are a SaaS founder trying to interview churned users quickly, or to run a small set of customer conversations this week, the guide is what makes those interviews comparable. Without one, every conversation wanders, and you cannot tell whether a new answer is a new theme or just a new wording. With a guide that is too long, or too leading, you will rush the questions that matter and hear agreement instead of a story.

You do not need a research team to write one. Name the decision, write a short core of open questions about a recent event, plan a few follow-ups, and use the same core with every person you invite. [The Mom Test](the-mom-test.html) is a useful check on the wording: ask about past behavior, not whether they like your idea. The playbook below is for a founder who needs a guide they can actually use, not a document that sits in a folder.

## Playbook

### 1. When to write the guide, and what it is for

Write the guide after you name the decision, and before you send the first invite. Typical founder decisions: why people cancelled, how a buying committee chose you or someone else, whether a job is painful enough to warrant a product change, or why a feature is unused. The guide is the list of topics and questions that will help you make that decision. It is not a list of everything you are curious about.

If you write the guide after the first two conversations, you will spend those interviews discovering what you should have asked, and you will not be able to compare them with the later ones. If you keep adding questions after every interview, you no longer have one study. A small revision is fine: drop a question that is not paying off, or add a follow-up under a question that keeps coming back thin. Change the core only when you are ready to treat the next set as a new round.

The guide also sets the tone. An opener that says you want to learn about a recent cancel, a recent evaluation, or the last time they did a job will get a story. An opener that pitches your product, or asks them to rate it, will get a review. Write the opener as carefully as the questions.

### 2. Who the guide is for, and who you will invite

The guide has two audiences: the people you interview, and the people who will run those interviews. For the first group, invite people you can already reach who recently lived the event you are studying: cancelled customers, buyers from deals you just closed, or people who recently did the job. Frank AI Researcher does not recruit participants. For the second group, write the guide so a teammate, a founder, or an AI moderator can follow it without you sitting in.

If more than one person will run interviews, share the same guide. That includes a success lead who is calling a cancelled account, a founder who is talking to a design partner, and an AI-moderated voice interview that asks your questions from a link. Comparability comes from a consistent core, not from the same human asking every time.

Keep the guide short enough that a cancelled customer, or a busy buyer, can get through it without rushing. A founder who is trying to interview churned users quickly is usually competing with silence, not with a desire for a 45-minute session. Five to eight core questions, each with a planned follow-up, is a compact shape many teams can actually finish. That is a practical range, not a rule that fits every study.

When a committee was involved, the same guide should still work for more than one seat, with room to ask what each person cared about. Do not write a totally different guide for every role unless you are running separate studies.

### 3. What to put in the guide

A compact guide often has four parts:

1. A short opener that states the purpose and asks for a recent event (Walk me through the last time...).
2. A core set of open questions, often around five to eight, in a stable order.
3. Planned follow-ups, such as What happened next? or Who else was involved?
4. A close that asks what you missed, and thanks the person.

Every core question should earn its place against the decision you named. If you are studying churn, you need the trigger, the people involved, what they tried, and what they use now. See [churn interview](churn-interview.html). If you are studying a lost or won deal, you need the evaluation, the committee, the alternatives, and what almost changed the outcome. See [win-loss interview](win-loss-interview.html). If you are still forming the problem, you need a recent attempt at the job, the workaround, and where they got stuck. See [discovery interview](discovery-interview.html) and [Jobs to be Done](jobs-to-be-done.html).

Keep the wording open and non-leading. "Did you cancel because of price?" is a [leading question](leading-question.html). "Walk me through what happened in the days before you cancelled" leaves room for price, for a champion leaving, and for a workflow that broke. Check the draft with [The Mom Test](the-mom-test.html), or with the [Mom Test question checker](../tools/mom-test-checker/), before you publish the study.

You can draft a guide by hand, start from a template, or use the [customer interview guide generator](../tools/customer-interview-guide-generator/). Ready starting points include the [customer interview questions template](../customer-interview-questions-template.html), the [30 customer interview study templates](../templates/), and the [customer interview script generator](../tools/customer-interview-script-generator/). If you are converting a survey into conversations, the [survey to interview question converter](../tools/survey-to-interview-converter/) is a useful first pass. You still have to edit. A generated or templated guide that you have not taken yourself is not ready to send.

### 4. What to do with the guide, and with the answers

Take the interview yourself before anyone else does. If a question feels awkward in your own mouth, or in a test with Frank, fix it now. Then use the same core with each person you invite. Read the guide as a map, not as a script. If someone tells you something surprising, follow it, then return to the next core question so the interviews stay comparable. The follow-up is part of the guide.

When the interviews are done, the guide is also the frame for the readout. Group answers under the same questions and themes, attach one exact quote to each theme, and open the transcript before a quote travels. If a question produced thin answers every time, that is a finding about the guide, not only about the customers. Tighten the wording, or drop the question in the next round.

Do not change the wording mid-round and then treat all of the answers as one dataset. If the study is not working, pause, revise the guide, and start a new set. Watch for [saturation](saturation.html): when new interviews largely repeat themes you already have, you can stop, or you can tighten the question and run another short round. A founder who keeps adding one more conversation with a slightly different guide will never feel done, and will not have a comparable set.

Share the guide and the readout with the people who will make the product or success decision. A well-written guide that nobody else can find is not doing its job.

## Approaches compared

An interview guide can be used in more than one kind of conversation, and some approaches do not use a real guide at all. A live call with a short guide is still the right tool when you need to follow a messy story in real time. An async survey can reuse some of the same topics, but it is not an interview. An AI-moderated voice interview can run your guide without a shared calendar. A rigid script, or no guide at all, will cost you either candor or comparability.

| Approach | Cost | Speed | Candor and depth | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Live call with a written guide | High time cost to schedule and run each conversation, plus the time to write and share the guide | Limited by calendars | High depth when the interviewer is skilled, uses the guide as a map, and follows a surprising answer. Comparability depends on whether everyone actually uses the same core | When the story is messy, you need to build a relationship, or someone needs to show you their screen |
| Async survey | Low time cost per person once the questions are written | Fast to send to many people | Shallow on why. Useful for counts. The "guide" here is a questionnaire, not a conversation with follow-ups | When you already understand the topic and need to measure how many people do or think something. A survey is not an interview guide |
| AI-moderated voice interviews that use your guide | You spend time writing and testing the guide, and inviting people you already have, not on scheduling each call. [Frank AI Researcher](https://www.hifrank.ai/?utm_source=frank-mcp-docs&utm_medium=glossary&utm_campaign=interview-guide) is one option. Frank asks the questions you put in the study and follows up during the voice interview | People can talk when they have time, and interviews can run in parallel, which helps a founder who needs conversations quickly | Deeper than a survey, more consistent than ad hoc chats, because the core questions stay stable. A skilled human is still better for messy, relationship-heavy interviews, or when someone needs to show you their screen | When you have a focused guide, can already reach the people, and need comparable stories without booking every call |
| A rigid script with no room to follow up | Low planning cost, high quality cost | Can feel fast in the moment, because the interviewer just reads | People give short, cautious answers, and you miss the story that was not on the page | Almost never for research interviews. A script can help a new interviewer start, but the follow-up is part of the work |
| No guide, or a new set of questions every time | Looks cheap, then becomes expensive when you cannot compare answers | The first chats can start immediately | You may hear a vivid story, and you will not know whether the next person would have said the same thing, because you asked something else | A single exploratory conversation can help you write the guide. It is not a substitute for a study |

A founder can use these in sequence: an unguided conversation or a survey to find the decision, a written guide for a short round of interviews, and an AI-moderated voice interview when people will not book a call. The mistake is to skip the guide and then try to treat a pile of different conversations as one finding.

## Common mistakes

- Writing twenty questions and rushing the ones that matter. A shorter, consistent core is easier to compare.
- Changing the wording in every interview, then trying to treat the answers as one dataset.
- Reading the guide as a script and ignoring a surprising answer. The follow-up is part of the guide.
- Starting with [leading questions](leading-question.html), or with a pitch, then asking for an honest story.
- Writing the guide after most of the interviews are already done, then wondering why the first conversations are hard to compare.
- Sending a generated guide you have not taken yourself. If the questions feel awkward in a test, they will feel worse to a customer.
- Treating a survey questionnaire as an interview guide. A survey counts. An interview follows a story.

## FAQ

### How do I write an interview guide if I am a SaaS founder interviewing churned users quickly?

Name the decision, then write a short core of open questions about the recent cancel, downgrade, or last session. Ask about the trigger, who else was involved, what they had already tried, and what they use now. Keep five to eight core questions, plan a follow-up under each, and use the same core with every person you invite. You can start from a template or the [customer interview guide generator](../tools/customer-interview-guide-generator/), but you should take the interview yourself before you send it.

### How many questions should an interview guide include?

There is no fixed number that fits every study. A compact guide often has around five to eight core questions, in a stable order, with planned follow-ups. That is enough to cover a focused decision without rushing. If you need a much longer list, you may be asking more than one decision at a time.

### Should every interview use the same guide?

Yes, if you want to compare answers in one study. Keep a consistent core, and leave room to follow a surprising answer. A small revision is fine. If you change the core questions, treat the next conversations as a new round rather than as one dataset.

### Can Frank use an interview guide I write?

Yes. Frank AI Researcher asks the questions you put in a study and follows up during an AI-moderated voice interview. You still choose the questions, take the interview yourself before you share it, and invite people you already have. Frank does not recruit participants.

### How is an interview guide different from a script?

A guide is a planned list of topics or questions, in a consistent order, with room to follow up. A script is a set of lines to read. Research interviews need the follow-up. If you only read the page, you will miss the story that was not already written down.

### Do I need a research team to write an interview guide?

No. A SaaS founder can write a short guide around one decision, check it for leading questions, and reuse it with people they can already reach. A research team helps when the question is broad, several people will interview, or you need help designing a longer study. You do not need a research team to start.
