# Discovery interview

> **TL;DR.** A discovery interview is a conversation meant to understand a problem, a workflow, or a job someone already has, before you shape a product. It focuses on recent, real events rather than reactions to a solution you have already decided to build. The output is a picture of the problem and current workarounds, not a yes on your idea. A product manager can run these with people they can already reach, as long as the guide stays open and the questions stay about what the person did last time.

## For product managers

Product managers use discovery interviews when the job is still to understand the problem, not to test a screen. That is a different task from a usability test, a roadmap interview that pitches a solution, or a survey that counts how many people say they have a pain. If you are choosing what to build next, entering a new segment, or trying to explain why a feature is ignored, you need stories about recent work: what started the job, what they tried, where they got stuck, and what they use now.

A PM on a small team can already reach some of the right people: current users, people who signed up and stalled, customers who asked for help, or contacts in a segment you are exploring. ## Playbook

### 1. When to reach out

Run discovery interviews while you are still forming the problem, before the solution has hardened into a plan you are mainly trying to confirm. Good moments: you are entering a new segment, a feature is unused and you do not know what job it was meant to do, support keeps hearing the same workaround, or you need to decide whether a job is painful enough to warrant a product change. Poor moments: you already have a design and you mainly want people to praise it, or you are asking whether they would use something they have not lived with.

Reach out to people who recently did the job or felt the problem, while that event is still easy to recall. "Tell me about the last time" is a more useful opening than "Do you ever have this problem?" A person who finished the job last week can walk you through the steps, the tools, and the people they involved. A person who last did it a year ago will give you a smoothed story, or a guess.

You can also run discovery after you have shipped, when the question is still about the job in someone's life. If the question has become "Can they complete this task in the product?", that is a usability test. See [usability test vs customer interview](usability-test-vs-customer-interview.html).

### 2. Who to invite

Invite people you can already reach who recently did the job or felt the problem. Product managers often start with current users, signup lists, support conversations, and people in a segment they already sell to or hope to sell to. Frank AI Researcher does not recruit participants. If you cannot name who you will invite, you do not yet have a discovery study. You have a wish for a panel.

Aim for a mix, not only people who already love your category. Current users can tell you how they work around the product. People who ignored you, chose something else, still do the job in a spreadsheet, or tried once and left can tell you what the product does not cover, or where the job stalled. If you only interview fans, you will hear compliments and still miss the workflow.

When the job involves a committee or a handoff, try to hear more than one role: the person who feels the pain, the person who has to approve a change, and anyone who currently owns the workaround. A PM who interviews only the enthusiastic champion will hear a clean problem statement and miss the person who actually does the work at 5 p.m.

There is no fixed interview count that makes a discovery study complete. Keep the question focused, interview people who actually did the job, and stop or tighten the question when new conversations largely repeat themes you already have. That judgment is [saturation](saturation.html).

### 3. What to ask

Keep the guide open enough to follow a story, and tight enough that you can compare interviews. Anchor every question in a recent event. [The Mom Test](the-mom-test.html) is a useful check: ask about past behavior in a real situation, not whether they like your idea. Compliments, hypothetical answers, and promises about the future are weak evidence.

A short guide for a product manager can stay close to this pattern:

1. Walk me through the last time you had to deal with [problem or job]. What started it?
2. What did you do first, and what did you try after that?
3. Where did you get stuck, and what did that cost you in time, money, or coordination?
4. Who else was involved, and how did you decide what to do?
5. What do you use now, including a workaround, and what is still awkward about it?

Useful follow-ups stay with the event: "What happened next?", "How did you choose that?", "What did you try before this?", and "If this failed, what did you do?" If they mention a tool, a spreadsheet, or a colleague, ask how that became the path, rather than asking whether they would switch to yours. If they start reviewing your idea, steer back to the last time they did the job.

Leave out "Would you use this?", "Do you have this problem?", and "How disappointed would you be if we built X?" Closed or hypothetical questions hide the real workflow. If you want a ready starting guide, use the [customer interview guide generator](../tools/customer-interview-guide-generator/) with a discovery goal, check the wording with the [Mom Test question checker](../tools/mom-test-checker/), or start from [customer interview questions for product managers](../customer-interview-questions-for-product-managers.html) or [customer interview questions for founders](../customer-interview-questions-for-founders.html).

A [Jobs to be Done](jobs-to-be-done.html) frame can help you hear the progress they were trying to make, and the outcome they wanted, rather than a feature list. The interview is still about their last attempt, not a job statement you wrote in advance.

### 4. What to do with the answers

The output of discovery is a clearer picture of the problem and the current workarounds, plus a decision about what to do next. It is not a stack of compliments, and it is not a yes on your idea. Read the interviews together. For each person, capture the trigger, the steps they took, the workaround they use now, who else was involved, and what was still awkward. Then look across the set for repeated stories.

A useful readout for a product team is short: the job people were trying to get done, the workarounds that already exist, where they get stuck, what you will build or change, and what you will not do on the basis of a single encouraging conversation. Attach one exact quote to each theme, and open the transcript before a quote goes into a roadmap doc. A summary can turn "I pasted data into a spreadsheet because the export was late" into "they want a dashboard."

Watch for [saturation](saturation.html). If new interviews keep adding new kinds of workarounds, your question may be too broad, or you may be mixing segments. Tighten who you invite, or split the job, rather than treating a mixed pile as one finding. If the same story keeps coming back, you can act, or you can move to a different method: a survey to see how common the job is, or a usability test once you have a design to put in front of people.

Share the readout with the people who will make the product decision. Discovery that stays in a notebook, or that is treated as validation because two people were polite, does not change the roadmap.

## Approaches compared

Product managers can learn about a job in several ways. A live call is still the right tool when you need to follow a messy story or watch someone show their work. An async survey is a fit when you already understand the job and need counts. An AI-moderated voice interview is a fit when you want comparable stories and cannot schedule every conversation. Ticket and event review can show you where people struggle in a product you already have, but it will not explain a job they do entirely outside it. A usability test answers a different question once a design exists.

| Live call | High time cost to schedule and run each conversation | Limited by calendars | High depth when the interviewer is skilled. People can go off-script or show their work. Good for a messy problem or a relationship you need to build | When the problem is messy, you need to build a relationship, or someone needs to show you their screen |
| Async survey | Low time cost per person once the survey is written | Fast to send to many people | Shallow on why. Useful for counts, such as how many people do a job or how often it happens. Weak for stories and workarounds you have not already named | When you need to measure how many people do or think something you already understand, or to find people to interview |
| AI-moderated voice interviews | You spend time on the guide and invitations, not on scheduling each call. A human interviewer is still better for messy, relationship-heavy discovery, or when someone needs to show you their screen | When a product manager has a focused question, can already reach the people, and needs comparable stories without booking every call |
| Support tickets, sales notes, and product events | Low extra cash if you already have them | Immediate, because the material already exists | They show where people struggled in your product or what they asked for. They miss jobs that never touch your product, and they are written in the company's words as often as the customer's | As a way to choose a discovery question, or to see where a workflow breaks in a product you already have. Many product managers use these in sequence: tickets or events to spot a pattern, a discovery interview to hear the story, a survey if they need to know how common the job is, and a usability test once a design exists. The mistake is to skip the interview and treat a feature request or a survey score as discovery.

## Common mistakes

- Pitching the solution, then asking people to rate it. That is not discovery.
- Asking Would you use this? or Do you have this problem? Closed or hypothetical questions hide the real workflow.
- Talking only to people who already love your category, and missing the ones who currently ignore it.
- Stopping after two encouraging conversations and treating that as validation. Look for repeated stories, and notice when new interviews stop changing the picture. See [saturation](saturation.html).
- Treating a usability test, or a walkthrough of a design, as a discovery interview. One asks whether people can use something. The other asks whether the job matters.
- Writing the finding in your own product language. Keep the customer's words, and check each quote in the transcript.
- Inviting people who have never done the job, then asking them to imagine it.

### When should I use a survey instead of a discovery interview?

Use a survey when you need counts: how many people do a job, how often it happens, or how they would choose among options you already understand. Use a discovery interview when you need the story: what they did last time, what they tried, and what was missing. Many teams use a survey to find a pattern, then interview a smaller set of people about that pattern.

### Can I use discovery interviews to validate an idea I have already decided to build?

You can learn something, but only if you still ask about the person's past behavior and current workarounds, not whether they like your idea. A compliment or a promise to use the idea is weak evidence. If you already have a design, a usability test answers whether people can use it; a discovery interview answers whether the job matters.

### Can a product manager run discovery interviews without a research team?

Yes, if you can already reach people who recently did the job or felt the problem. Write an open guide about a recent event, invite those people, and look for repeated stories. A research team helps when the question is broad, the audience is hard to reach, or you need facilitation on a messy, relationship-heavy conversation. Frank does not recruit participants.

### How is a discovery interview different from a usability test?

A discovery interview asks about a real event or job in someone's life, often with no design in front of them. A usability test watches someone try a task in a product or design. Frank runs voice interviews, not usability tests, and it does not run image or prototype tests.
