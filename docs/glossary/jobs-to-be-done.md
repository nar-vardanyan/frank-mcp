# Jobs to be Done (JTBD)

Jobs to be Done (JTBD) is a way of understanding why someone hires a product to make progress in a particular situation. The unit of analysis is the job, the circumstances around it, and the outcome they want, not a demographic or a feature list. The approach is associated with Clayton Christensen and Bob Moesta.

Updated November 27, 2026.

## How it's used

Product and research teams use Jobs to be Done when they need to understand the progress a person is trying to make, not only how they describe a product. It is a fit for early discovery, for idea validation, and for interviews after someone switches to or from a product.

In a JTBD interview you ask about a recent time the person tried to make that progress: what was going on, what they tried, what almost stopped them, and what they hired instead. You invite people you already know or can already reach. Frank AI Researcher does not recruit participants.

## Example questions

These questions ask about a recent, real attempt to make progress:

1. Walk me through the last time you tried to [job]. What was going on around you?
2. What had you already tried before that, and what was missing?
3. What almost stopped you from switching, or from starting at all?
4. What did you hire instead, including a workaround, a spreadsheet, or doing nothing?
5. After you chose something, what still felt unfinished?

Avoid questions such as 'Would you use a tool that does X?' or 'How much would you pay for this idea?' Those ask for a reaction to your solution, not a story about the job.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a persona or a demographic as the job. Age or job title can be context, but they are not the progress the person is trying to make.
- Asking people whether they like your idea, instead of asking about a recent time they tried to make the progress.
- Forcing every answer into a pre-written job statement during the interview. Write the statement after you have several concrete stories.
- Assuming a switch means the new product is a full hire. People often stitch together several workarounds.
