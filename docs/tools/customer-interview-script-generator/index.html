How to use this interview script

  1. Write down the one decision this research should change, such as "do we fix onboarding or pricing first?" Cut any question that does not help that decision.
  2. Interview people who did the thing recently. Someone who cancelled last week remembers why. Someone who cancelled last year tells you a story.
  3. Keep the core questions the same across interviews so you can compare answers, but let the conversation follow the person.
  4. Spend your time on the probes. The first answer is usually the polite one. The useful part comes after "Tell me more about that" and "What happened next?"
  5. Write up each interview the same day, then look for patterns after five or six conversations.

What makes a good customer interview question

  • Ask about the past, not the future. "Tell me about the last time you..." gets a real story. "Would you use...?" gets a guess, and usually a polite yes.
  • Keep it open. Start with what, how, tell me about, or walk me through. Avoid questions that can be answered with yes or no.
  • Don't lead. "How frustrating was the setup?" assumes it was frustrating. "How did setup go?" does not.
  • Ask one thing at a time. Double questions get half an answer.
  • Get specific. When someone says "it was confusing," ask what exactly they were trying to do and what they saw.
  • Get comfortable with silence. Wait a few seconds after an answer. People often add the most honest part when you don't jump in.

Example questions for each research goal

Churn interview questions
  • When did you first start thinking about cancelling? What was happening at the time?
  • What were you hoping the product would do for you when you signed up?
  • What are you using instead now, if anything?
  • What, if anything, would have made you stay?

Win-loss interview questions
  • What made your team start looking for a solution in the first place?
  • Which options did you consider, and how did you narrow them down?
  • Who was involved in the final decision, and what did each person care about?
  • What was the moment you knew which way you were going to go?

More on this: win/loss interviews.

Onboarding interview questions
  • Walk me through the first day you used the product. What did you do first?
  • Where did you get stuck or have to stop and figure something out?
  • When did it first feel useful, if it did?
  • What did you expect to be able to do that you couldn't?
Pricing interview questions
  • How did you decide this was worth paying for?
  • What were you paying for before, in money or in time?
  • When you looked at the pricing page, what did you look at first?
  • At what point would it start to feel too expensive, and why there?
Feature discovery interview questions
  • Tell me about the last time you tried to get this job done. What did you do, step by step?
  • What's the most annoying part of how you do it today?
  • What have you tried to fix that, and how did it go?
  • If this problem disappeared tomorrow, what would change for you?
Product-market fit interview questions
  • How would you feel if you could no longer use the product? Why?
  • What is the main benefit you get from it?
  • What would you use instead if it disappeared?
  • Who do you think would get the most out of it?

Frequently asked questions

What is a customer interview script?

A customer interview script, also called an interview guide, is the plan for one conversation with a customer: how you open, a few warm-up questions, the core questions tied to your research goal, follow-up probes for each one, and how you close. It keeps interviews consistent so you can compare answers across people.

How many questions should a customer interview have?

For a 20 to 30 minute interview, plan 6 to 8 core questions and expect to spend most of the time on follow-ups. A short list you can go deep on beats a long list you rush through. This generator lets you pick 15, 30 or 45 minutes and sizes the guide to match.

Should I read the script word for word?

No. Use it as a guide. Keep the core questions the same across interviews so you can compare, but follow the participant's story, skip questions they already answered, and spend time where the answer is surprising.

What makes a good customer interview question?

Good questions are open, ask about something that already happened, and do not suggest the answer. Ask "Tell me about the last time you..." instead of "Would you use...?" or "Do you like...?". People are better at describing what they did than predicting what they will do.
