# AI-Moderated Customer Interviews for Founders: Frank vs Survey Tools

**In short:** For most founding teams, survey tools are the right way to measure how many people feel a certain way, or to track a score over time. AI-moderated voice interviews are the right way to learn why someone bought, stalled, or cancelled, because the interviewer can ask a follow-up. The two methods do different jobs, and they work well together. This page is a fair comparison of those jobs, written for founders who already have customers, users, or a list they can invite themselves.

## What founders usually need to decide

Early product work tends to produce two kinds of questions:

- **How many?** What share of trial users would miss the product if it went away? Has onboarding satisfaction changed this quarter? Which of three known reasons do people pick most often for not upgrading?
- **Why?** What happened the week someone cancelled? Where did they get stuck the first time they tried to get value? Why did they choose you, another option, or nothing?

Survey tools are built for the first kind. An interview is built for the second. Nielsen Norman Group places surveys, among common UX methods, as the one that is both quantitative and attitudinal: a good fit for questions like "what percentage of users feel confident after filing?", and a poor fit for questions like "why are people abandoning the cart?" ([Should You Run a Survey?](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/should-you-run-a-survey/)).

If you cannot say how a finding would change a decision (a pricing change, an onboarding step, a feature you will or will not build), tighten the question before you pick a tool.

## When a survey is the right tool

Choose a survey when the decision depends on a number you can compare over time or across groups, and you already know the likely answers well enough to list them.

Good founder survey questions look like:

- What share of current users would be disappointed if the product went away?
- How satisfied are people with onboarding this month, compared with last month?
- Which of these three reasons do people pick most often for not upgrading?

Keep the questions closed enough to tally. Nielsen Norman Group notes that surveys can be a cost-effective way to collect quantitative, attitudinal data, and that they pair well with qualitative methods, but they are easy to misuse when the real question is qualitative or behavioral ([Should You Run a Survey?](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/should-you-run-a-survey/)).

A short survey is also a reasonable first pass when you mainly need a count, not a story. If "price" or "missing feature" is enough to choose a next step, you may not need interviews yet.

## When an AI-moderated voice interview is the right tool

Choose interviews when you need the story behind a number, or when you do not yet know which answers to put on a survey.

Good founder interview questions look like:

- Walk me through the moment you decided to cancel. What almost made you stay?
- Tell me about the first time you tried to get value from the product. Where did you get stuck?
- Why did you choose this option, the other option, or nothing? What almost tipped it the other way?

A live 1:1 call is still the right choice when you need a relationship conversation or a shared screen. An AI-moderated voice interview is a better fit when you want that same kind of "why" conversation from many of your own customers, and you cannot schedule a call with each of them. Participants open a [link](https://www.hifrank.ai/docs/navigating-your-ai-interviewer-dashboard?utm_source=github&utm_medium=docs&utm_campaign=frank-mcp) or a [website widget](https://www.hifrank.ai/docs/website-widget-embedding-frank-on-your-site?utm_source=github&utm_medium=docs&utm_campaign=frank-mcp), talk when they have time, and the AI interviewer asks follow-ups. The method is still an interview, so the sample-size logic is still saturation, not a percentage. What changes for a small team is the cost of running enough conversations to reach that point: interviews can run in parallel, because nobody on your team has to moderate each one live.

## When to use both

Interviews and surveys answer different parts of the same decision. Nielsen Norman Group describes the split simply: quantitative methods show measurable patterns at scale; qualitative methods give you the why ([Mixed-Methods Research](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mixed-methods-research/)).

Two patterns work well for founding teams:

1. **Interview first, then survey.** Use interviews to learn the real reasons and the customers' words. Then write a short survey that offers those reasons, so you can see how common each one is.
2. **Survey first, then interview.** Use a survey or your product analytics to see where something is dropping. Then interview the people in that group to find out why.

In the mixed-methods example cited above, a discovery-phase study planned at least 40 survey participants and about 10 interviews, with the interview count depending on when new themes stopped appearing. Treat those figures as planning guidance from that example, not as a universal rule.

A product-market fit study is a familiar version of this: a survey question can tell you how many users would miss the product, and interviews tell you why. If you already have survey answers, tickets, or call notes to organize, a research repository may also help; that is a different job from running the interviews.

## A simple way to choose

Ask two questions before you start:

1. **Is this a "why" question or a "how many" question?** "Why are trial users not activating?" is an interview. "What percentage of users feel confident after completing onboarding?" is a survey.
2. **Do I already know the possible answers?** If you don't, interview first. If you do, and you need a count, survey.

If the answer would not change a decision this month, wait. Founders get more from a short study tied to one decision than from a broad form that tries to do both jobs at once.

## FAQ

### Should a founder use surveys or AI-moderated customer interviews?

Use surveys when you need a comparable number from a larger group. Use AI-moderated voice interviews when you need to understand why someone bought, stalled, or cancelled. Many teams use both: interviews to learn the reasons, surveys to measure how common they are.

### Does Frank replace survey tools?

No. Frank runs AI-moderated voice interviews. It does not turn a small set of conversations into a statistically tidy percentage. If you need a number you can track, keep the survey or look at analytics.

### Can I run interviews if I already have a survey tool?

Yes. Keep the survey for measurement. Use interviews when a score, a dropdown, or an open text box does not tell you what to change. You can also interview a segment you already identified in a survey, such as people who said they would be disappointed if the product went away, or people who picked "other" as their cancel reason.

### How many interviews does a founder need?

Continue until new conversations stop adding themes. In one widely cited study, saturation occurred within the first 12 interviews for a relatively homogeneous group, with the basic elements of themes present as early as 6. Run more if you compare several segments, and do not treat interview counts as a market percentage.

### Does Frank find customers for me to interview?

No. Frank does not recruit participants. You share the interviewer link or website widget with customers, users, or prospects you can already reach.
