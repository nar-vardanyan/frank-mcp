# How to Validate a Startup Idea with Customer Interviews

**In short:** Validate a startup idea by interviewing people about a job they already do, not by asking whether they like your idea. Ask about the last time they hit the problem, what they tried, what they use now, and what they already pay in money or time. You find those people yourself and invite them. ## What these interviews can and cannot prove

A useful discovery interview is about the present and the recent past: a job, a workaround, a tool they already pay for. It is a poor test of a pitch. People are polite about hypothetical products, and "I would use that" is not evidence they will.

These interviews can tell you:

- Whether the problem happened recently, and how often
- What they already do about it, including "nothing" or a spreadsheet
- What they have already tried and abandoned
- Who else is involved in the decision

They cannot tell you that your solution will work, that people will pay your price, or that you have product-market fit. For that later question, see [How to measure product-market fit with customer interviews](measure-product-market-fit-with-customer-interviews.md). Do not use these conversations to test a prototype or an image if you want to learn about the current job; that is a different kind of test, and it is not what Frank does.

Ask about a specific recent episode rather than a general opinion ([Nielsen Norman Group, The Critical Incident Technique in UX](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/critical-incident-technique/)). Open questions let people answer in their own words ([Open-Ended vs. Closed Questions](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/open-ended-questions/)).

## A six-step way to run them

1. **Write the job, not the pitch.** One sentence: I want to learn how [who] currently [does this job], so I can decide [whether to build / what to build first]. If you cannot state it without naming your product, rewrite it.
2. **Decide who you can already reach.** People who recently described the problem, joined a waitlist and talked about the job, or already use a workaround are stronger than strangers you have not spoken to. Frank does not recruit a panel for you. If you have nobody to invite, start with your network, communities you already take part in, or a recruiting service. Do not expect a tool to find them.
3. **Write 6 to 8 open questions about the last time.** A starting list is below, and a longer copyable set is in [Customer interview questions template](customer-interview-questions-template.md). Cut "Would you use my idea?" and "How much would you pay?"
4. **Hold the interviews.** Live calls work well early, when you want to hear hesitation and ask a follow-up in the moment. AI-moderated voice interviews work when you already have people who will take a link, and you want a consistent guide without scheduling each call. See [How to run customer interviews](how-to-run-customer-interviews.md).
5. **Listen for four things:** the trigger (what started the last attempt), the struggle (where they got stuck), the alternative (what they use now), and whether they have already looked for something better.
6. **Decide in writing.** After new conversations stop adding themes, write a one-page readout: what the job actually is, what people already do, what you will build or skip, and what you still do not know. In one well-known study, thematic saturation occurred within the first 12 interviews for a relatively homogeneous group; broader or mixed groups often need more ([Guest, Bunce & Johnson, 2006](https://www.sfu.ca/~palys/GuestEtAl-2006-HowManyInterviewsAreEnough.pdf)).

## Starting questions

Copy and edit this list. Replace [job] with the customer's words.

1. Tell me about the last time you tried to [job]. What were you trying to get done?
2. What did you try first? What did you try after that?
3. Where did you get stuck, or what took longer than you expected?
4. What do you use now? What do you like about it, and what do you work around?
5. What do you already pay to get this done, in money, time, or favors?
6. When was the last time you actively looked for a better way? What did you search for, or who did you ask?
7. Walk me through what happened the last time this went badly.
8. If this problem disappeared tomorrow, what would change in your week?
9. Who else gets involved when this goes wrong?
10. Is there anything I should have asked?

Do not follow a compliment about your idea. If the person asks what you are building, you can say you are trying to understand this job first, then go back to their last attempt.

## When this is a poor fit

If you cannot name a recent job, you are still at the pitch stage. If you have nobody you can already reach, recruiting or your own outreach has to come first. If you need to watch someone use a screen or react to a design, a human session is a better fit; Nielsen Norman Group found AI-moderated interviews work best as a complement to human moderation ([AI-Moderated Interviews](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-interviewers/)). And if you already have users and want to know whether they would miss the product, use the product-market fit workflow instead of idea-validation questions.

## FAQ

### Can I validate an idea if I have no customers yet?

Yes, but you still have to find people who already have the job. Interview them about the last time they tried to do it. Frank will not find them for you.

### Should I show people my idea?

Not in this workflow. You are trying to learn about the current job. A pitch or a prototype changes what people tell you, and Frank does not run prototype or image tests.

### Can Claude or ChatGPT validate the idea on their own?

No. Simulated personas are not your customers. An assistant can help draft questions and summarize real transcripts once an interview tool is connected. You still invite the people and check the quotes.

### How many interviews should I run?

Continue until new conversations stop adding themes. In Guest, Bunce and Johnson's study, saturation occurred within the first 12 interviews for a relatively homogeneous group, with the basic elements of themes present as early as 6. Broader questions or mixed audiences often need more.

### Does Frank recruit participants?

No. You invite people you can already reach.
