# How to Run User Interviews in Multiple Languages

**In short:** To run user interviews in multiple languages, write one core guide, adapt it (not just translate it) for each language with a native speaker, and let people answer in their own language. An AI interviewer can run the conversations in each language without a bilingual moderator, but a native speaker should still check the guide and key quotes.

## Why language matters in interviews

People describe problems most precisely in the language they use every day. Asking a Spanish-speaking customer to explain a frustration in English gets you a shorter, flatter answer, and you lose the words they would use to search for a solution.

Language is also only one layer. Jakob Nielsen describes three levels of international concern: displaying the user's language and notation, translating the interface in a usable way, and matching the user's culture, including how people do business and communicate. His advice for products used abroad is to test with users in those countries, using a translator or local consultant if needed ([Nielsen Norman Group, International Usability Testing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/international-usability-testing/)). The same applies to interviews: a literal translation of your questions can still miss.

## Step by step

### 1. Pick the decision and the markets

Write one sentence: we will interview [who] in [which languages or markets] to decide [what]. Choose languages by where the decision matters, such as markets you are entering or segments with unusual churn, not by how many languages you can support.

### 2. Write one core guide in your working language

Aim for 6 to 8 open questions about recent behavior, each with a follow-up. Keep the core identical across markets so answers can be compared. Avoid idioms, sports metaphors, and product jargon that will not survive translation. Open-ended questions work best because they let people answer in their own words ([Nielsen Norman Group, Open-Ended vs. Closed Questions](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/open-ended-questions/)).

### 3. Adapt the guide with a native speaker

Have someone fluent in each language review the questions for meaning, tone and formality, not only grammar. Ask them to flag anything that sounds like a sales pitch or that would be rude or odd locally. Check names for features and plans: some stay in English, some are localized. The W3C's internationalization guidance is a good primer on why language and locale are more than translation ([W3C, Localization vs. Internationalization](https://www.w3.org/International/questions/qa-i18n)).

### 4. Test each language yourself, or with a native speaker

Take the interview in each language before you invite anyone. Listen for unnatural phrasing, wrong formality and questions that come out longer than you expected. Fix the guide, then test again.

### 5. Invite customers in their own language

Write the invite in the participant's language and say plainly which language the interview will be in. Use channels you already have: customer email, in-app messages, or a [website widget](https://www.hifrank.ai/docs/website-widget-embedding-frank-on-your-site) on a localized page. Frank does not recruit participants, so you need customers you can already reach in each market. If you don't have them yet, use a local partner or a recruiting service first.

### 6. Analyze across languages, check quotes in the original

Read transcripts per market first, then compare. Group answers into themes across languages, but check each theme you act on against the original-language quote, ideally with a native speaker. Translated summaries can flatten nuance, such as politeness that hides a strong complaint. Stop when new interviews in a market stop adding themes; for a fairly similar group, saturation often comes within about a dozen interviews ([Guest, Bunce & Johnson, 2006](https://www.sfu.ca/~palys/GuestEtAl-2006-HowManyInterviewsAreEnough.pdf)).

## Common mistakes

- **Translating the guide word for word.** Meaning, not wording, should match.
- **Forcing one language.** If people answer in a second language, you get shorter answers.
- **Merging markets too early.** Read each market separately before comparing.
- **Trusting a translated quote alone.** Check the original before you put it in a deck.
- **Treating counts as market size.** Interviews explain why; use a survey to measure how many ([Nielsen Norman Group, Should You Run a Survey?](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/should-you-run-a-survey/)).

## When AI-moderated interviews are a poor fit

Nielsen Norman Group found AI interviewers work best as a complement to human moderation, and they are a weaker fit for messy discovery, deep domain knowledge, or showing a workflow on screen ([AI-Moderated Interviews](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-interviewers/)). In a market where the relationship matters a lot, such as a few large enterprise accounts, use a local human moderator.

## FAQ

### How do I interview users who speak different languages?
Write one core guide, adapt it with a native speaker for each language, and let people answer in their own language. Use a bilingual moderator, an interpreter, or an AI interviewer that runs in each language.

### Can an AI interviewer run interviews in languages my team doesn't speak?
Yes. Nielsen Norman Group lists multilingual interviews without translators as a good use of AI-moderated interviews. Have a native speaker review the guide and check the key quotes.

### Should I translate transcripts before analysis?
Translate to compare across markets, but check every quote you will act on in the original language. Translations can lose tone and nuance.

### Do I need separate studies per language?
Keep the core questions the same so you can compare. Whether that is one study or one per market depends on your tool; either way, read each market separately before merging themes.
